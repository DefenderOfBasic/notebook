Peer review is an epistemic shortcut. If you are able to validate the truth of the paper yourself (like just test it/firsthand) you don't need it. If you can't, you trust someone or something to do it.

You cannot avoid all epistemic shortcuts. But you can detect the good ones.

As [@kdcc100](https://x.com/kdcc100/status/1935302179188822455) says, there are two categories when you are providing an epistemic shortcut to someone else:

1. **"trust me"**  - just give them the answer, hide the complexity 
2. **"do you see why this is true"** -  show them how you know, teach them

The first is easy, but fragile, and easily abused. 
The second grows their awareness, and ability to contribute back.


# Epistemic shortcuts are necessary, but some grow your awareness, & others shrink it (2)

Option 2 is ALWAYS the better answer. You should ALWAYS listen
